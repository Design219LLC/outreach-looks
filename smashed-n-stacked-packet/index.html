First · Who did this, and why

ATTN: James Mann

The smash burgers left the truck and landed in East Village. Someone searching for that door still lands on Facebook or Toast.

Public listings put Smashed N' Stacked at 1020 E 6th St Unit 4, Little Rock, AR 72202, phone (501) 291-2353. The door fascia reads Unit 4; a few directories still say Unit D. Press coverage of the brick-and-mortar move lists hours as Wednesday through Saturday, 11am to 5pm — marked on the look as DIRECTORY, call to confirm, not as an owner-locked permanent board.

What a hungry person finds today is a strong Facebook page (facebook.com/smashednstacked) carrying the photos and the conversation, and ordering tools that are not a place you own. There is no finished site sitting at an address under your name.

I build websites and the software under them — the part customers see and the systems that keep a shop running. On my own time I built a finished look from photographs already public on your Facebook: the foil stack, the cheese pull, the Unit 4 garage door, the truck-era portrait. Voice on the hero is the one already in the room: Your favorite burger just got a new home.

Open it on a phone. Call dials you. Directions open maps to Unit 4. Nothing is published. Nothing is owed. If you want it, we put it on an address you own and point search there. If you do not, you have lost nothing.

01 · The front page

Your plate, your name, your street.

The first screen is the foil-wrapped smash with the cheese pull — food first, not a door photo standing in for the product. Beside it sit the name, the line about the new home, the DIRECTORY hours chip, and the two buttons someone actually needs: Call and Directions. No menu to dig through before the phone number. Unit 4 and East Village show up as soon as they scroll one breath.

02 · Every screen

Computer, tablet, phone.

The same site rearranges itself for whatever opens it. On all three sizes the hours chip, the Call button, and Directions sit on the first screen before anyone scrolls. Nothing important is buried behind a hamburger menu.

On a phone there is one extra touch: a bar pinned to the bottom with Call, Hours, and Directions. It stays put while someone reads, so the number is never more than one thumb-reach away.

04 · Being found

What search engines see.

A website is not only a page for people — it is also what Google reads when somebody in Little Rock types smash burger near me or Smashed N' Stacked East Village. This one was built to be read properly.

1

Machine-readable shop facts

Name, street address (Unit 4), phone, and the fact that this is a restaurant sit in the page in the format Google publishes for local results. That is what lets hours and a map pin show up beside a listing once the site is live and claimed.

2

Every page titled and described

Home and Visit each carry their own title and summary, so a result reads like an invitation instead of a bare web address.

3

Shares that look like something

Pasted into Facebook or a text, the link can open with a photograph and a proper description instead of a lonely blue line.

4

Written in plain structure

Real headings in a sensible order, and a written description on every photograph. Good for search for the same reason it is good for a blind customer.

5

Fast, and built for phones first

Google measures both and counts them. This is a light site built for a phone from the ground up, not a desktop page squeezed down.

An honest word about search. None of this is a magic switch. Results build over weeks, and for a local burger shop the single biggest factor is a claimed, filled-in Google Business Profile pointed at an address you own. The site makes that profile stronger. Anyone who promises the top spot is selling something else.

05 · Site and social together

Facebook is the conversation. The site is the address.

You rent the Facebook page. You would own the website. They do different jobs, and they are stronger pointed at each other.

Your Facebook page is already doing the living work — the foil drops, the move to Unit 4, comments from people who followed the truck. The site does not try to replace that. It links straight to facebook.com/smashednstacked and leaves specials and day-of noise where they belong.

What Facebook cannot do is be the first door for someone who has never heard of you. A driver looking for smash burgers in East Village does not start inside Facebook — they search Google, or they ask the phone out loud. A page inside a social network is largely invisible to that search. A website is exactly what it is looking for.

Facebook does

Today

Specials, sold-out stacks, a chalk-door update, the crowd after the move. Things that change and want a comment thread.

The site does

Always

Hours posture, Unit 4 address, phone, what the shop is. The facts somebody needs with one hand on the wheel.

Together

A loop

Search finds the site, the site sends them to Facebook to follow you; posts link back to the site, which lifts it in search. Each one feeds the other.

There is a practical safety net too. Pages get locked, hacked, or caught by a rule change, and there is no support line that answers. If that ever happened to yours, the address, the phone, and the Visit facts would still sit at a place you own.

07 · What it opens up

The door this puts in the wall.

None of the following is built, and none of it is required for the site to do its job. Once a burger shop owns its own address — especially one that just stepped off a truck into a brick room — each item stops being a second-vendor chase and becomes a small addition from the same bench. Site and software, one pair of hands. Think about this slowly.

First

Knowing what happens

How many people looked, what they tapped, what hour, whether they were already in East Village or two exits away. Numbers instead of a gut feel after the lunch rush.

First

A board you update yourself

Swap a stack name, park a sold-out note, or post today’s foil special from your phone. No waiting on a developer to change a price.

First

Counter or window QR

Same facts on the glass or the napkin holder, always current, never a reprint run when the truck special becomes a shop special.

Next

Call-ahead that sticks

A clean path for “I’m five minutes out, smash two” that lands as one message instead of a missed ring during the rush.

Next

Rewards that remember

A punch card that lives on a phone for the people who followed you from the truck lot to Unit 4 — and tells you who actually comes back.

Next

Catering and office drops

A form for the game-day tray or the downtown lunch order, arriving as one clear ticket instead of a half-heard voicemail.

Later

Inventory that counts down

Patties, buns, foil, sauces — what is on hand and what ran out last Saturday. The end of the clipboard by the flat-top.

Later

Gift cards

Sold from the site, redeemed at the counter. Holidays and “you have to try this place” mostly run themselves.

Later

A list you own

Customers who ask to hear from you by text or email, reachable without paying a platform to talk to your own regulars.

Later

Reviews, gathered

A gentle nudge to a happy customer at the right moment, pointed where it counts for local search.

The order matters more than the list. Analytics first, because it tells you which of the others is worth doing. Everything after that becomes a decision made with evidence instead of a hunch from the pass.
